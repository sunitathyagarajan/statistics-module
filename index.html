Replicates and Uncertainty

Every number that comes off a balance, a burette or a blood analyzer is a best estimate with a spread around it. This module shows how to describe that spread, how to carry it through a calculation, and how to decide when two results really differ.

Part 1

Key terms

Formulas use n for the number of replicate measurements and xi for each individual result. Each card ends with how the term shows up at the lab bench.

Describing a set of replicates

Population and sample

The population is every measurement you could ever make on the material; its true mean is μ and its standard deviation is σ. A sample is the handful of replicates you actually made, described by x and s.

Where it matters: A few tablets tested from a batch of millions decide whether the whole batch can be sold.

At the bench: your five titrations are a sample. μ is the concentration you are trying to find.

Mean x

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}{n} \]

The arithmetic average. It is the best single estimate of μ when errors are random.

Where it matters: Doctors average several blood pressure readings rather than trusting a single one.

At the bench: the value you report for a set of replicate titrations or absorbance readings.

Median

n odd: the middle value of the sorted data
\[ \tilde{x} = x_{(n+1)/2} \]
n even: the average of the two middle values
\[ \tilde{x} = \frac{x_{n/2} + x_{n/2 + 1}}{2} \]
Subscripts give positions in the data sorted from low to high

Sort the data and take the middle value; with an even n, average the two middle values. One wild value barely moves it.

Where it matters: Reporting typical lead levels across city water taps, where a few very high taps would inflate the mean.

At the bench: a quick check. If mean and median differ a lot, look for an outlier.

Mode

The value that occurs most often. It is rarely useful for continuous lab data, where exact repeats are uncommon, but appears with counted or rounded data.

Where it matters: Finding the most common particle size in a pharmaceutical powder, which affects how fast a tablet dissolves.

At the bench: the most common reading on a digital meter that only shows whole units.

Range w

\[ w = x_{\max} - x_{\min} \]

The simplest measure of spread. It depends only on the two extreme values.

Where it matters: A quick check on duplicate analyses: a large range between the two flags a problem at once.

At the bench: the denominator of the Q test for outliers.

Deviation from the mean di

\[ d_i = x_i - \bar{x} \]

How far one result sits from the mean. Deviations always sum to zero, which is why they are squared before averaging.

Where it matters: Showing which replicate pulls a result away from the rest before you investigate it.

At the bench: the building block of variance and standard deviation.

Variance s²

\[ s^2 = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2}{n - 1} \]

The average squared deviation, using n − 1. Variances add when independent errors combine, which makes them central to propagation and to the F test.

Where it matters: Combining the uncertainty from weighing, diluting and reading an instrument, because variances add.

At the bench: the F test compares the variances of two methods.

Standard deviation s, σ

\[ s = \sqrt{\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^2}{n - 1}} \]

The square root of the variance, in the same units as the data. It describes how much a single measurement typically scatters. σ (dividing by N) is the population value; s estimates it from a sample.

Where it matters: Clinical labs run control samples daily and flag a run when a control falls outside mean ± 2s.

At the bench: the main number for precision. Calculators label it s, sx or σn−1.

Degrees of freedom ν

\[ \nu = n - 1 \]
for one data set

The number of independent deviations. Once x is fixed, only n − 1 of the deviations are free to vary. Critical values of t and F depend on ν.

Where it matters: Choosing the right t value: duplicates (ν = 1) need t = 12.7, so they give very wide intervals.

At the bench: 4 titrations give ν = 3 when you look up t.

Relative standard deviation RSD, CV

\[ \text{RSD}\ (\%) = \frac{s}{\bar{x}} \times 100 \]
\[ \text{RSD}\ (\text{ppt}) = \frac{s}{\bar{x}} \times 1000 \]

The standard deviation as a fraction of the mean, so precision can be compared across very different magnitudes. In clinical labs it is called the coefficient of variation (CV).

Where it matters: Method validation: drug assays are often required to show an RSD of 2% or less on replicates.

At the bench: a good titration has an RSD of a few parts per thousand (ppt).

Standard error of the mean sx

\[ s_{\bar{x}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

The uncertainty in the mean, which is smaller than the scatter of single results. Four times as many replicates halves it.

Where it matters: Planning how many replicates to run: going from 3 to 12 titrations cuts the uncertainty of the mean in half.

At the bench: why you run triplicates rather than a single titration.

Error, accuracy and precision

Accuracy

How close a result is to the true or accepted value. It is checked against a certified reference material (CRM) or a standard.

Where it matters: Clinical labs must analyze outside proficiency-test samples correctly to keep their accreditation.

At the bench: analyzing a CRM and comparing your mean with its certificate.

Precision

How close replicate results are to each other, measured by s or RSD. Repeatability is precision under the same conditions; reproducibility is precision across analysts, days or labs.

Where it matters: Transferring a method to another lab: reproducibility shows the method works outside the lab that developed it.

At the bench: you can be precise and still wrong. See Example C2.

Absolute and relative error

Absolute error
\[ E = \bar{x} - x_{\text{true}} \]
Relative error
\[ E_{\text{rel}} = \frac{\bar{x} - x_{\text{true}}}{x_{\text{true}}} \times 100\% \]

Error has a sign: a negative error means the result is low.

Where it matters: Checking that a tablet’s measured drug content is within the allowed percentage of its label claim.

At the bench: “% error” on a graded unknown.

Systematic (determinate) error

A reproducible bias in one direction with a cause you can find and fix: an uncalibrated pipette, an impure standard, a wrong indicator, a drifting baseline.

Where it matters: A balance that reads 2 mg low makes every weighing in the lab wrong until someone checks it with standard weights.

At the bench: hurts accuracy. Repeating the measurement does not average it away. The t test against a known value detects it.

Random (indeterminate) error

Unpredictable scatter from many small sources: reading a meniscus, electrical noise, temperature fluctuation. It is equally likely to be high or low.

Where it matters: Explains why two glucose readings from the same finger stick differ by a few mg/dL.

At the bench: hurts precision. Averaging more replicates reduces its effect on the mean.

Gross error and outliers

A large, one-off mistake such as a spilled sample or a misread burette. The result is an outlier: a value far from the rest. Use a test, not a hunch, to discard it.

Where it matters: Environmental labs must justify any discarded result before reporting contaminant levels to regulators.

At the bench: the Q test decides whether a suspect value may be rejected.

Significant figures

The digits you know for certain plus the first uncertain digit. Uncertainty sets them: round the uncertainty to 1 (or 2) significant figures, then round the result to the same decimal place.

Where it matters: Forensic and clinical reports must not claim more digits than the instrument can support.

At the bench: a burette read to 0.01 mL gives 24.13 mL, not 24.1 or 24.130.

Uncertainty and confidence

Uncertainty u

Absolute uncertainty (same units as x)
\[ x \pm u \]
Relative uncertainty (no units)
\[ \frac{u}{x} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{u}{x} \times 100\% \]

A range that probably contains the true value. Absolute uncertainty has units (± 0.02 mL); relative uncertainty is a fraction or percentage (± 0.08%). It comes from instrument tolerances (Type B) or from the scatter of replicates (Type A).

Where it matters: A blood alcohol result near the 0.08 g/dL legal limit is judged together with its uncertainty.

At the bench: a Class A 25 mL pipette is 25.00 ± 0.03 mL.

Propagation of uncertainty

Addition or subtraction: \(y = a + b\) or \(y = a - b\)
\[ u_y = \sqrt{u_a^2 + u_b^2} \]
Multiplication or division: \(y = a \times b\) or \(y = a \div b\)
\[ \frac{u_y}{y} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{u_a}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{u_b}{b}\right)^2} \]
Logarithm: \(y = \log x\)
\[ u_y = 0.434\,\frac{u_x}{x} \]
Antilogarithm: \(y = 10^{x}\)
\[ \frac{u_y}{y} = 2.303\,u_x \]

How the uncertainties of the inputs combine into the uncertainty of a calculated result. Add absolute uncertainties for sums and differences; add relative uncertainties for products and quotients. Always add in quadrature (squared).

Where it matters: Preparing drug dilutions or standards: it shows which step limits the accuracy of the final concentration.

At the bench: the uncertainty in a molarity from mass and volume. Try the calculator in Part 5.

Normal (Gaussian) distribution and z

\[ z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \]

The bell-shaped curve followed by random errors. 68.3% of results fall within ±1σ, 95.4% within ±2σ and 99.7% within ±3σ. z says how many σ a value lies from μ.

Where it matters: Many clinical reference ranges, such as for serum potassium, cover the central 95% (about ±2σ) of healthy people.

At the bench: quality-control charts in clinical labs flag a control outside ±2σ.

Student’s t

When σ is unknown and estimated by s from a few replicates, the bell curve is replaced by the wider t distribution. t shrinks toward z as ν grows.

Where it matters: Any check based on a handful of replicates, where σ is unknown, relies on t instead of z.

At the bench: t = 2.776 for 5 replicates at 95% instead of z = 1.960.

Confidence interval (CI)

\[ \mu = \bar{x} \pm \frac{t\,s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

A range around x expected to contain μ with a stated probability. In the long run, about 95 of every 100 intervals built this way contain μ.

Where it matters: Drug trials report a 95% CI for the treatment effect. If the interval includes zero, the benefit is not established.

At the bench: the “± 0.04 mL” at the top of this page.

Confidence level and significance level α

\[ \alpha = 1 - \text{confidence level} \]

A 95% confidence level means a 5% chance (α = 0.05) of declaring a difference that is really just random error.

Where it matters: A factory may choose 99% confidence before rejecting an expensive batch, to reduce false alarms.

At the bench: most lab reports use 95%. Clinical decisions sometimes demand 99%.

Hypothesis tests and calibration

Null hypothesis H₀

The assumption that there is no real difference: the two means are equal, the variances are equal, the suspect point belongs. A test asks whether the data give strong enough evidence to reject H₀.

Where it matters: Quality control assumes a batch meets its label claim until the data show strong evidence that it does not.

At the bench: “My method has no systematic error.”

Test statistic and critical value

The test statistic (t, F or Q) is computed from your data. The critical value comes from a table for your confidence level and n. If the calculated value is greater than the critical value, reject H₀.

Where it matters: Deciding whether a cheaper, faster method gives the same results as the official method.

At the bench: tcalc = 4.50 > tcrit = 2.262 means the difference is significant.

p-value

The probability of getting a difference at least this large by random error alone if H₀ were true. A p-value below α (for example 0.05) means a significant difference.

Where it matters: Clinical trials use p-values to show that a drug’s effect on patients is larger than chance alone would produce.

At the bench: spreadsheet t-test functions return p rather than tcrit.

Calibration curve

Least-squares line
\[ y = mx + b \]
Concentration of an unknown
\[ x = \frac{y - b}{m} \]

Instrument response (absorbance, peak area, emission) plotted against standard concentrations. The least-squares line gives slope m and intercept b; an unknown is found from x = (y − b)/m. R² near 1 shows linearity but does not prove accuracy.

Where it matters: Blood glucose analyzers and breathalyzers are calibrated against known standards before their results are trusted.

At the bench: Beer’s law plots in UV-vis and AAS.

Blank

A sample containing everything except the analyte. Its signal is subtracted from samples and standards to correct for reagents, the cuvette or the matrix.

Where it matters: In trace-metal analysis, impurities in the acid or glassware can exceed the analyte unless a blank is subtracted.

At the bench: zeroing a spectrophotometer with a reagent blank.

Limit of detection and quantitation LOD, LOQ

Limit of detection
\[ \text{LOD} = \frac{3\,s_{\text{blank}}}{m} \]
Limit of quantitation
\[ \text{LOQ} = \frac{10\,s_{\text{blank}}}{m} \]

The lowest concentration that can be told apart from the blank (LOD), and the lowest that can be measured with acceptable precision (LOQ). sblank is the standard deviation of blank replicates; m is the calibration slope.

Where it matters: Drinking-water testing: deciding whether arsenic is truly absent or only below what the method can detect.

At the bench: deciding whether trace lead in a water sample is “not detected” or “detected but below LOQ”.

Definitions and formulas follow standard analytical chemistry texts and measurement guides. See references 1–7.

Materials & engineering

Case 1 · Challenger: the flights left off the chart

The data
Damage to the rubber O-rings that seal the shuttle’s booster joints, on the 23 earlier flights whose boosters were recovered, with the launch temperature of each. Rubber stiffens and seals poorly in the cold.
The mistake
The night before launch, the review looked only at the 7 flights that had O-ring damage. Damage showed up at 53 °F and at 75 °F, so temperature seemed not to matter. The 16 flights with no damage were left out as carrying no information.
The outcome
Challenger launched on 28 January 1986 with a forecast of 31 °F, 22 °F colder than any earlier flight. An O-ring seal failed and the shuttle broke apart 73 seconds after liftoff. All seven crew members died.
The lesson
Results that show “no effect” are still data. With all 23 flights plotted, the trend is clear.

Data: O-rings with erosion or blow-by on each flight, from Dalal, Fowlkes and Hoadley (1989) as archived by UCI. Account of the decision: Rogers Commission report (1986). References 13–16.

Medical

Case 2 · Vioxx: a missing control and three missing heart attacks

The data
The VIGOR trial randomly assigned 8,076 patients with rheumatoid arthritis to the painkiller rofecoxib (Vioxx) or to naproxen. It compared stomach side effects and also recorded heart attacks.
The mistake
The 2000 paper reported heart attacks in 0.4% of rofecoxib patients and 0.1% of naproxen patients, and presented this as naproxen lowering the risk. With no placebo group, the trial could not show which drug caused the difference. Three more heart attacks on rofecoxib, known to at least two authors before publication, were left out.
The outcome
Vioxx stayed on the market until a placebo-controlled trial (APPROVe) found that it nearly doubled the rate of heart attacks and strokes. Merck withdrew it in September 2004. A study of over 2 million person-years later found that naproxen does not protect the heart.
The lesson
A difference between two treatments does not tell you which one changed. That takes a control: a placebo in medicine, a blank in chemistry. And no result may be dropped because it is inconvenient.

Data: VIGOR heart-attack counts from the journal editors’ 2005 expression of concern (the 2000 paper gave only percentages); APPROVe rates from Bresalier et al. (2005). References 17–21.

Part 6

Which test, and in what order?

Every test below follows the same logic: assume there is no real difference (H₀), compute a statistic from your data, and compare it with a critical value from a table. If the calculated value is larger, the difference is too big to blame on random error at your chosen confidence level. When you compare two data sets, run the tests in this order:

Q test

Is one value in a set so far from the others that it may be discarded?

F test

Do two data sets have significantly different precision (variance)?

t test

Is a mean different from a known value, or are two means different from each other?

Test 1

The Q test (Dixon’s test for outliers)

Use the Q test when one result in a small set (3 to 10 values) looks suspiciously far from the rest. Sort the data, then compare the gap between the suspect value and its nearest neighbor with the range of all the data.

\[ Q_{\text{calc}} = \frac{\text{gap}}{\text{range}} = \frac{\left|x_{\text{suspect}} - x_{\text{nearest}}\right|}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} \]

If Qcalc > Qcrit for your n and confidence level, the suspect value may be rejected. Otherwise it must be kept. Apply the test once. Never reject a value just because it spoils your precision, and never test again after removing one point. Some textbooks use the Grubbs test instead; the reasoning is the same.

Sources: Dean and Dixon (1951); critical values from Rorabacher (1991). References 9–10.

Medical example

Five replicate measurements of calcium in one control serum (mg/dL): 9.42, 9.47, 9.45, 9.44, 9.71. Can 9.71 be rejected at 95% confidence?

Sorted: 9.42, 9.44, 9.45, 9.47, 9.71
\(\text{gap} = 9.71 - 9.47 = 0.24\)  \(\text{range} = 9.71 - 9.42 = 0.29\)
\(Q_{\text{calc}} = \dfrac{0.24}{0.29} = 0.828\)  \(Q_{\text{crit}}\ (n = 5,\ 95\%) = 0.710\)

0.828 > 0.710, so reject 9.71. Report the mean of the remaining four: 9.445 mg/dL with s = 0.021 mg/dL. Keeping the outlier would have inflated s almost sixfold, to 0.120.

Test 2

The F test (comparing precision)

Use the F test to decide whether two sets of results have significantly different variances: two instruments, two analysts, an old and a new method. It also tells you which form of the two-mean t test to use.

\[ F_{\text{calc}} = \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2} \]
Put the larger variance on top, so \(F \ge 1\)

Degrees of freedom are ν1 = n1 − 1 for the numerator and ν2 = n2 − 1 for the denominator. If Fcalc > Fcrit, the precisions are significantly different. Following most analytical chemistry textbooks, this page uses Fcrit at the 95% level from a one-tailed table.

Sources: Harris and Lucy; Miller and Miller; NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook. References 1, 3, 6.

Medical example

A clinic compares two glucometers by measuring the same control solution seven times on each (mg/dL).
Meter A: 101, 97, 104, 99, 103, 95, 100 → sA = 3.18
Meter B: 100, 101, 99, 100, 102, 99, 101 → sB = 1.11

\(F_{\text{calc}} = \dfrac{3.185^2}{1.113^2} = \dfrac{10.14}{1.238} = 8.19\)
\(\nu_A = 6,\ \nu_B = 6\)  \(F_{\text{crit}}\ (6,\ 6,\ 95\%) = 4.28\)

8.19 > 4.28, so meter A is significantly less precise than meter B. Both have a mean near 100 mg/dL, so the meters seem equally accurate. Their precision is what differs.

Test 3

The t test (comparing means)

Student’s t test asks whether a difference between means is larger than random error can explain. There are three situations, each with its own formula. In every case, if tcalc > tcrit (two-tailed, at your confidence level and degrees of freedom), the difference is significant.

Sources: Student (1908); Harris and Lucy; Skoog et al. References 1, 2, 8.

Case 1 · Comparing a mean with a known value

\[ t_{\text{calc}} = \frac{\left|\bar{x} - \mu_{\text{known}}\right| \sqrt{n}}{s} \]
\[ \nu = n - 1 \]

Use it to check a method for systematic error by analyzing a certified reference material or standard.

Chemistry example

A CRM is certified at 10.00 mg/L Cu. Student A finds 9.62, 9.65, 9.60, 9.64 (x = 9.6275, s = 0.0222, n = 4).

\(t_{\text{calc}} = \dfrac{\left|9.6275 - 10.00\right| \sqrt{4}}{0.0222} = \dfrac{0.3725 \times 2}{0.0222} = 33.6\)
\(t_{\text{crit}}\ (\nu = 3,\ 95\%) = 3.182\)

33.6 > 3.182, so Student A has a systematic error. Student B (x = 10.0325, s = 0.174) gets t = 0.37, which is not significant.

Case 2 · Comparing two independent means

\[ t_{\text{calc}} = \frac{\left|\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2\right|}{s_{\text{pooled}}} \sqrt{\frac{n_1 n_2}{n_1 + n_2}} \]
\[ \nu = n_1 + n_2 - 2 \]
Pooled standard deviation
\[ s_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2\,(n_1 - 1) + s_2^2\,(n_2 - 1)}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}} \]

Use it for two separate sets of replicates: two labs, two methods on the same material, two samples. Run an F test first. If the variances are not significantly different, pool them as above. If they are, use the unequal-variance (Welch) form, \[ t = \frac{\left|\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2\right|}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}} \] with ν from the Welch–Satterthwaite equation.

Medical example

Two hospital labs measure cholesterol in the same control serum (mg/dL).
Lab X: 198, 202, 195, 201, 199, 203 → x = 199.67, s = 2.944, n = 6
Lab Y: 206, 209, 204, 211, 207 → x = 207.40, s = 2.702, n = 5

F test: \(F = \dfrac{2.944^2}{2.702^2} = 1.19 < F_{\text{crit}}(5,\ 4) = 6.26\), so pool
\(s_{\text{pooled}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{8.667 \times 5 + 7.300 \times 4}{9}} = 2.839\)
\(t_{\text{calc}} = \dfrac{\left|199.67 - 207.40\right|}{2.839} \sqrt{\dfrac{6 \times 5}{11}} = 4.50\)
\(t_{\text{crit}}\ (\nu = 9,\ 95\%) = 2.262\)

4.50 > 2.262, so the labs disagree. At least one of them has a bias, which matters for a patient whose result straddles a treatment threshold.

Case 3 · Paired t test

\[ d_i = x_{B,i} - x_{A,i} \]
Subtract in the same order (method B minus method A) for every specimen
\[ t_{\text{calc}} = \frac{\left|\bar{d}\right| \sqrt{n}}{s_d} \]
\[ \nu = n - 1 \]

Use it when each specimen is measured once by both methods and the specimens differ from each other, such as different patients or different water samples. Working with the differences removes the large specimen-to-specimen variation.
