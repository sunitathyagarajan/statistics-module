Significant Figures

The digits you write down are a claim about how well you measured. Significant figures are all the digits you know for certain plus the first one you had to estimate. This page covers which digits count, how to carry them through a calculation, and 20 problems to practice on.

Which digits count

Six rules cover every number you will write in lab. The examples are colored the same way throughout this page.

3 · Leading zeros never count

They only locate the decimal point. 0.0052 g is 5.2 mg: same measurement, two digits.

4 · Trailing zeros after a decimal point count

3 4

Writing 2.50 says you measured the hundredths place and found 0. Dropping it to 2.5 throws away information.

5 · Trailing zeros in a whole number are ambiguous

2, 3 or 4
2 3

Use scientific notation to say which zeros you measured. The power of ten never counts.

6 · Exact numbers have unlimited significant figures

Counted items (3 replicates, 12 tablets), defined conversions (1000 mL = 1 L, 1 in = 2.54 cm) and coefficients in a balanced equation are not measured, so they never limit a result.

Example: the mean of 3 titrations is the sum divided by 3. The 3 is exact.

Reading an instrument: record every certain digit plus one estimate

A 50-mL burette is marked every 0.1 mL. The bottom of the meniscus is past 20.3 but not at 20.4, a little more than halfway. You read 20.36 mL: three certain digits and one estimated digit.

The same idea sets the digits for every instrument. An analytical balance that displays 0.0001 g gives four decimal places; a 25-mL Class A pipette is written 25.00 mL; a top-loading balance that reads to 0.1 g gives one decimal place.

Writing 20.4 throws away a digit you could estimate. Writing 20.362 claims a digit no one can see.

Part 2

Rules for calculations

A calculator returns as many digits as it can display. The rules below decide how many of them mean anything.

Adding and subtracting: count decimal places

Keep the decimal place of the least precise number. Line the numbers up; the first column with an unknown digit ends the answer.

12.11 18.0? + 1.013 ──────── 31.123 → 31.1

18.0 says nothing about the hundredths place, so the sum cannot either. Subtraction can lose figures: 47.632 − 46.1 = 1.5, four and three figures in, two out.

Multiplying and dividing: count significant figures

Keep as many significant figures as the input with the fewest.

4.56 × 1.4 = 6.384 → 6.4

Why: 1.4 could be anything from 1.35 to 1.45, so the product lies between 6.16 and 6.61. The 6 is certain, the next digit is already a guess, and the digits after it are noise.

Logarithms and antilogarithms

Digits after the decimal point in a log equal the significant figures in the number. The digit before the point only sets the power of ten.

[H⁺] = 2.0 × 10⁻³ M (2 s.f.) pH = −log(2.0 × 10⁻³) = 2.70 (2 decimals) 10−2.70 = 2.0 × 10⁻³ (2 s.f.)

Several steps: keep guard digits

Work the steps in order and note how many figures each step keeps, but carry one or two extra digits. Round only the final answer.

(8.21 + 0.4) × 1.135 = 8.61 × 1.135 (tenths → 2 s.f.) = 9.77235 → 9.8

The subscript 1 is the guard digit: kept for the arithmetic, not counted. Rounding every step adds up small errors that can change the last digit.

Rounding

Look at the first digit you drop. Below 5, leave the last kept digit alone. Above 5, or 5 followed by other nonzero digits, round up.

Exactly 5: Harris recommends rounding to the nearest even digit (2.45 → 2.4, 2.35 → 2.4), so that rounding up and down balance over many results. Many general chemistry courses always round up. None of the problems on this page depend on that choice.

When you know the uncertainty, it decides

Round the uncertainty to one significant figure (two if its first digit is 1). Then round the result to the same decimal place.

24.1327 ± 0.0412 mL → 24.13 ± 0.04 mL

Significant figure rules are a quick estimate. A real uncertainty, from a standard deviation or from propagation of uncertainty, always wins.

Rules follow Harris and Lucy (ch. 3) and Skoog et al. (ch. 6); writing numbers and rounding follows NIST SP 811. References 1, 2 and 22.
